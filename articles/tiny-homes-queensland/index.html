Tiny homes in Queensland

What to check before you buy, build or place a tiny home.

Tiny homes can create practical new housing options—but the right pathway depends on the property, the home and how you intend to use it.

A tiny home may be a small, prefabricated dwelling, a modular or relocatable home, a backyard pod, a granny flat—or something on wheels. That is why the answer is rarely a simple yes or no.

It is not one simple rule.

Queensland does not have a single planning definition for a “tiny home”. The official guidance instead looks at the home’s form, the intended use and the site. A temporary home on wheels may be treated more like a caravan and local laws may apply. A home intended for permanent on-site accommodation may be assessed as a dwelling or secondary dwelling, with planning, building and plumbing considerations depending on the proposal.

The key point: a tiny home being small, transportable or on wheels does not by itself settle the approval question.

Four questions to answer before you commit.

01

How will it be used?

Permanent living, family accommodation, rental, short-term stays, a home office or a business use can lead to different planning considerations.

02

What is already on the land?

An existing dwelling, the property’s zone, overlays and the number of dwellings proposed can change the likely pathway.

03

Will it be fixed or on wheels?

Whether the home is fixed to land, connected to services or intended to remain temporary makes a practical difference.

04

Can it meet the building pathway?

For a home intended as a dwelling, planning is only part of the picture. Building, plumbing, drainage, engineering, site access and supplier documentation may all matter.

Before you pay a deposit.

It is sensible to investigate the property before committing to a supplier, design or delivery date. Queensland’s building regulator recommends checking the relevant local planning requirements, speaking with an appropriate building certifier about the building pathway, and asking the supplier for documentation such as engineering and tie-down details.

This early work is especially useful where you are thinking about a secondary dwelling, where there is more than one home proposed, or where the home may be rented or used for short-term accommodation.

A useful starting point

Keep the address, council area, intended use, supplier information, proposed location on the property and service connection details together. These are the details that help turn a general question into a property-specific planning review.
